================================================================================
 STEP 1 - What is a slime chunk, and why do we care where they are
================================================================================
Minecraft's world is divided into 16x16-block "chunks." In every chunk, a
per-chunk formula (seeded by the world seed + the chunk's coordinates)
decides whether slimes are ALLOWED to spawn there underground: this makes it
a "slime chunk." Roughly 1 in 10 chunks qualify, seemingly at random per
world.

A "slime farm" works by standing (AAFK) at one spot and letting slimes spawn
in nearby slime chunks, then funneling/killing them automatically. More
eligible chunks near your AFK spot = more slimes = a better farm. So the
question this whole project answers is: **for a GIVEN world seed, which
(x,z) block coordinate has the most slime-eligible area around it, and which
SEED has the best such spot anywhere?**

================================================================================
 STEP 2 - Not every nearby chunk counts: the exclusion zone
================================================================================
Minecraft also has a rule: slimes cannot spawn within 24 blocks of a player.
So the 4 chunks immediately touching your AFK spot are ALWAYS dead - the
exclusion sphere covers them completely, no matter how "lucky" the seed is.
A naive scoring method that draws a simple disc/square around the AFK point
and counts all chunks inside is WRONG - it overstates every score by
counting those 4 guaranteed-dead chunks.

The correct eligible area is an ANNULUS (a ring, not a disc):

    24^2 < distance^2 <= 128^2

i.e. further than 24 blocks (outside the no-spawn sphere) but within 128
blocks (game's own simulation-distance-style cutoff). This ring covers 222
distinct chunk cells - THE fixed universe every score in this project is
measured against. It was verified block-for-block, chunk-for-chunk, against
the community-standard SlimeFinder.jar's own output (10/10 test rows).

================================================================================
 STEP 3 - The score itself: "cq"
================================================================================
cq = (eligible slime-spawnable BLOCKS within the annulus) / 256.

Why /256? A chunk holds 256 blocks (16x16) at any given Y layer, so
dividing by 256 expresses the score in "chunk equivalents" - a farm with
70.00 cq is exactly as good as 70 PERFECT chunks, even though in practice
no real farm hits 100% of any chunk (see step 4). This is more precise than
just counting "how many chunks are slime chunks," because it also captures
WHERE within each eligible chunk the good blocks land (a block near a
chunk's near edge vs. far edge is not equally valuable once you factor in
the distance-based annulus boundary cutting through it) - hence "blocks
divided by 256" beats plain "chunk count" as the real objective.

================================================================================
 STEP 4 - Chunks vs. cq: a chunk COUNT is a leaky proxy for the real score
================================================================================
It would be simpler to just count "how many of the 222 candidate chunks are
slime chunks" and rank by that. This project tried that first and learned
it's misleading: a chunk that's only PARTIALLY inside the eligible annulus
(near the ring's inner or outer edge) contributes fewer than 256 blocks even
if it fully qualifies as a slime chunk - so two spots with the same chunk
COUNT can differ by several cq depending on how well those chunks' USABLE
AREA packs into the ring. This project calls that packing efficiency, and it
falls the more chunks you have (see docs/REFERENCE.md's THE FRONTIER section for
the exact numbers) - which is why "more slime chunks" is not automatically
"a better farm," and why the real record search targets cq directly, not
chunk count.

================================================================================
 STEP 5 - The three tools, and which question each one answers
================================================================================
  hotspot_scan   "WHERE should I even look?"
                 Scans a wide area (thousands of candidate x,z spots) fast
                 and cheap, ranks them, and tells you which ones are worth a
                 real hunt. It does NOT know any real seeds yet - it works
                 by SAMPLING many random seeds at each spot and measuring
                 statistics (variance, hit rate) to guess which spots are
                 likely to produce a good seed if you actually hunt them.

  slime_gpu      "Brute-force search for an actual good SEED at one spot."
                 Given one (x,z) spot, tries millions/billions of seeds per
                 second on the GPU and records any that clear a minimum
                 slime-chunk-count threshold. This is "the hunt" - the
                 expensive, GPU-heavy step.

  slime_refine   "Now that I have a candidate seed, what is it REALLY
                 worth?" Computes the exact cq (down to individual blocks,
                 not just chunk count) for a seed at a spot, and can also
                 draw a picture (PNG) of the layout. This is the tool that
                 replaced needing SlimeFinder.jar at all.

Think of it as: hotspot_scan picks the neighborhood, slime_gpu knocks on a
lot of doors fast, slime_refine tells you what's actually behind each door
that answered.

================================================================================
 STEP 6 - Running the loop BY HAND, once, to see how the pieces connect
================================================================================
Build the three tools first (once per fresh checkout, or after any source
change):

    powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File build.ps1

What it does: compiles hotspot_scan.cu, slime_gpu.cu, and slime_refine.cpp
with nvcc, then runs every tool's --self-test and stops if any fails - so a
build that "succeeds" but silently self-tested against stale binaries
(a real gotcha this project hit once) can't slip through quietly.

  1. WHERE - scan a wide area for promising spots:

     hotspot_scan.exe --scan --center-x 0 --center-z 0 --range 10000 --z-all
       --var-min 26 --gate-top 300 --score-t 68 --name hunt

     What it does: samples many random seeds at thousands of candidate
     (x,z) spots around (0,0) out to 10000, restricted to the hot Z-lattice
     rows (--z-all, see step 8), and ranks them by how often a sample seed
     clears --score-t (68) chunks. --gate-top 300 keeps the top 300
     candidates from a coarse first pass before the more expensive ranking
     pass runs on them. --var-min 26 is a cheap pre-filter, cutting spots
     whose chunk-count variance is too low to ever be worth ranking.
     -> writes main\commands\commands_hunt.csv, one hunt command per
        candidate spot, best-ranked first, and PRINTS the exact commands
        for steps 2 and 3 below, already filled in per spot.

  2. HUNT - brute-force real seeds at one of those spots:

     slime_gpu.exe --seed-count 0 --center-x 3680 --center-z 6992 --m-chunks 24
       --fix-mask 0xFDE0F --fix-val 0x70802 --record-floor 70 --seeds-floor 70
       --seeds-out main\seeds\3680_6992.csv

     What it does: tries seeds on the GPU (--seed-count 0 means "until told
     to stop / give up," not "zero seeds"), restricted to the family
     --fix-mask/--fix-val derived for that spot by step 1 (see step 7),
     and writes any seed that clears --seeds-floor (70) chunks to
     --seeds-out. Do this once per spot from the commands CSV, or paste the
     runner hotspot_scan printed for you, which loops the whole file.

  3. WORTH - get the real, exact score for those candidate seeds:

     slime_refine.exe --seeds main\seeds\3680_6992.csv --center-x 3680
       --center-z 6992 --radius 6

     What it does: for every seed in that file, computes the EXACT cq (down
     to individual blocks, not just chunk count) AT THAT SAME (x,z) centre
     the hunt used - refining at the wrong centre silently gives ~25
     chunks, the random rate, with no error, so --center-x/--center-z must
     match step 2 exactly. --radius 6 also checks nearby chunk-aligned
     centres in case one scores slightly better than the exact spot hunted.
     Results land together in main\scores\<x>_<z>\, as <seed>.txt and
     <seed>.png - the score itself lives in the file content, not the
     filename. Both main\scores\ and main\seeds\ are WORKING folders,
     regenerated by any hunt/refine run - safe to delete anytime. Anything
     that scored 64+ is already auto-filed into main\records\, which is
     the only one of the two worth keeping around.

That's the entire loop. Everything else in this project (record_cycle.ps1,
hunt_pipeline.ps1, the ledger, the auto-abort logic, the climb step) is
AUTOMATION and OPTIMIZATION of exactly these 3 steps - not new concepts.

================================================================================
 STEP 7 - A "family": narrowing the search with a bitmask
================================================================================
A Minecraft seed is a 48-bit number that also fully determines EVERY slime
chunk in that world via a fixed formula (see slime_core.h). hotspot_scan's
--check / --scan doesn't just measure a spot's overall promise - it can
DERIVE which individual SEED BITS correlate with a high chunk count AT THAT
SPOT specifically, by testing many random seeds and comparing.

Fixing b of those bits to their derived "good" value carves out a FAMILY:
a residue class of exactly 2^(48-b) seeds, and (this is the important part)
NOTHING ELSE exists in that family. A tighter mask (more bits fixed) makes
each seed you sample MORE LIKELY to be a good one (higher hit rate) - but
also shrinks the total pool of seeds that could EVER be tried, exponentially
(each extra bit HALVES the family). Both of this project's real records came
from relatively loose 14-bit masks (2^34 = ~17 billion seeds), not the
tightest possible mask - a tight mask feels faster but can search itself
into a corner with nothing good left inside it. See README.txt's
"WHAT THE NUMBERS MEAN" section for the exact "winners" formula used to
reason about this trade-off precisely instead of by feel.

================================================================================
 STEP 8 - A genuine, exploitable geometric shortcut: the Z-lattice
================================================================================
It turns out real slime-chunk math (an artifact of how Minecraft computes
it — a 32-bit integer truncation quirk on one axis but not the other, see
docs/REFERENCE.md's THE COORDINATE LATTICE section for the actual code and the
exact bug) means GOOD SPOTS ARE NOT UNIFORMLY LIKELY
ACROSS THE MAP. Specifically, along the Z axis, only chunk-rows where
`chunkZ mod 97` is 48 or 49 can ever host one of this project's good spots
- that's 2 rows out of every 97, a ~48x reduction in the search space with
ZERO loss of coverage. --z-all in hotspot_scan generates exactly these rows
directly from the formula, so it can never "miss" the lattice by aiming at
the wrong anchor. X has no equivalent regularity - it has to be searched
directly.

This is why the scan command always includes --z-all: it is not a
convenience flag, it's the single most important structural fact this
project has found about where to even look.

================================================================================
 STEP 9 - Squeezing more out of a seed you already have: the high-bit climb
================================================================================
Once slime_gpu finds a promising seed, there's a cheap trick to see if a
CLOSE VARIANT of it scores even better, without hunting from scratch. It
relies on how Minecraft's random-number generator actually works
internally (a Linear Congruential Generator - LCG): flipping a HIGH bit of
the seed only ever perturbs the TOP few bits of the generator's internal
state (never the bottom ones - see docs/REFERENCE.md's THE HIGH-BIT CLIMB
section for the exact mechanism). That means a seed differing only in its
high bits renders an ALMOST-identical slime layout - not a fresh random
roll.

--climb-scan (fast, GPU, chunk-resolution) sweeps thousands of these
high-bit neighbors of a known seed and finds the promising ones; --climb
(slower, CPU, block-perfect) refines the best candidates to an exact score.
This single trick is how BOTH of this project's records were actually
found - not by hunting harder, but by climbing a known decent hit
(64.64 cq climbed to 66.21 cq is the most dramatic example on record).

Taken one step further, the TOP 16 bits of a 64-bit world seed turn out to
have EXACTLY zero effect on slime chunk generation anywhere in that world -
not "almost identical," genuinely identical. That means one known-good
seed has exactly 65,536 true duplicates, free, with no hunting or climbing
needed at all. Confirmed, not just theorized: refining the 66.21 record
seed against 4 top-bit-flipped variants, each in a separate process, all
landed on the exact same 16950 blocks / 66.21 cq / position - see
docs/REFERENCE.md's EXACT SEED DUPLICATES section for the proof and why it
matters.

================================================================================
 STEP 10 - Putting it all together: the automated record loop
================================================================================
record_cycle.ps1 is everything above (steps 6-9), automated and made
resumable:

    scan (step 6.1) -> triage-hunt every candidate spot (step 6.2, but
      auto-killed early if a spot looks dead - see main\families.csv ledger
      below) -> score with slime_refine (step 6.3) -> GPU-climb the best
      hits (step 9) -> family sweep the most productive family found
      (exhaustively checks if it's genuinely the best family AT that spot,
      not just a lucky sample) -> push more seeds at the current best-known
      family (keeps the anchor fresh) -> record everything.

Two pieces of PERSISTENT STATE make repeat runs (across days/sessions)
efficient instead of wasteful:
  main\cycles.txt      one line per cycle: what ran, what it found
  main\families.csv    the LEDGER - every (mask,val) family ever hunted, and
                        how many times, so a family that's already been
                        hunted twice with no improvement gets SKIPPED for
                        free next time ("mined") instead of re-hunted

Anything scoring 64+ cq gets auto-filed into main\records\ - one flat
folder, every file named "<cq>_<seed>.{txt,png}" so the best results sort
together - deduplicated by pattern so re-discovering the same layout from
a different seed doesn't look like 20 separate "finds."

The actual commands, in the order you'd use them:

    powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File record_cycle.ps1 -Range 100000 -Repeat 100

    What it does: runs the whole scan->hunt->score->climb->sweep->push loop
    above up to 100 times back to back, over a window +/-100000 blocks from
    the origin. Stops early ONLY on a genuine new best (see docs/REFERENCE.md's
    GOTCHAS for a bug that used to make it stop on FALSE new-bests, since
    fixed) - otherwise it just keeps going, skipping any family
    main\families.csv already marked "mined." -Range must be passed
    explicitly; the script's own default is 25000.

    powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File record_cycle.ps1 -Status

    What it does: prints current progress (cycle count, best cq found, an
    odds-of-a-new-record estimate from the measured tail fit) WITHOUT
    starting or touching anything - safe to run anytime, including while a
    -Repeat batch is running in another window.

    powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File stop_all.ps1

    What it does: the ONLY correct way to stop a running search. Kills the
    controlling script first, then any worker process, then VERIFIES the
    GPU actually goes idle before returning - killing the visible script by
    hand instead leaves a slime_gpu process orphaned on the GPU, which
    breaks the NEXT run with "CUDA error: unknown error."

================================================================================
 STEP 11 - The last, hardest question: is the model even right?
================================================================================
Everything above (cq, the frontier, the lattice, climbing) is a MODEL. It's
a very well-validated model - slime_refine's math is checked block-for-
block against SlimeFinder.jar - but that is validation against another
PROGRAM, not against Minecraft itself. There is one more tool for closing
that last gap: perimeter_setup.

Requires the Carpet mod (for /perimeterinfo) and WorldEdit (for the void
step) both installed on the world being tested - neither is optional.

The idea: build a physical rig, in the actual game, that makes the
question answerable directly. Void a large box around the AFK spot, lay a
single layer of obsidian at the world floor but ONLY in slime chunks
(everywhere else stays void), turn off mob spawning so live mobs don't eat
spawn positions, then read Carpet mod's `/perimeterinfo` - the game's own
count of spawnable positions around the player. Since every spawnable
surface in range is now slime-chunk floor by construction, that number IS
the spot's real slime capacity, directly comparable to what slime_refine
predicted for the same position.

    perimeter_setup.exe --seed <s> --center-x <x> --center-z <z>

    (The defaults build the reference rig: a 152-block half-width box, voided up
    to y=-40 in a superflat world, /tp to y=-50, Minecraft 1.20.4.)

    What it does: scans the same isSlimeChunk math already validated
    elsewhere in this toolchain across a box around (center-x,center-z),
    and prints the exact in-game commands to build the rig above - a
    teleport/gamerule setup, a WorldEdit void command, a
    slime-chunk floor, and a /give for the repeating perimeterinfo block
    you measure with. The floor alone typically needs 70-110 /fill commands,
    which is a real engineering problem on its own (chat and command blocks
    both cap how much can be pasted in one shot) - solved with a classic
    "one-command monolith" trick: one summon command that fires all the
    fills at once via a falling block landing an activator rail on top of
    itself, then deletes its own leftover blocks afterward.

The genuinely useful part of this step is the LAST thing it teaches: a
model prediction and an in-game measurement can legitimately disagree, and
the reason is almost always AFK height, not a bug. The model is computed
at y-offset 0 (standing level with the spawn surface) - but both the
24-block exclusion sphere and the 128-block despawn sphere are 3D, so
standing HIGHER than the floor shrinks the exclusion sphere's footprint
dramatically (24.0 blocks at y-offset 0 down to 6.9 blocks at y-offset 23)
while barely touching the despawn sphere. The two effects roughly cancel
in total area, but change WHICH specific chunks qualify - and since slime
chunks are scattered, not smooth, that's enough to flip a ranking between
two spots separated by a fraction of a cq. Confirmed on the real records:
the 66.04 spot measured 17,112 spawning spaces in game against a model
prediction of 16,905; a 66.21-cq spot has measured WORSE in game than the
66.04 one did. If a model number and an in-game number ever disagree, this
is the first thing to check, not the last.

================================================================================
 STEP 12 - How records are found now: the exact phase search
================================================================================
Steps 5-10 describe SAMPLING: try millions of seeds, keep the best. The
search that found every current record (70.82, 70.64, 70.34, 69.39, 69.30)
does not sample at all.

The trick (docs/PHASE_MODEL.txt): split a seed into a "family" (its
lowest 20 bits), a "class" (the next 15) and a top part (13 bits, 8,192
values). For one family and class, changing the top part moves EVERY chunk's
slime test in lockstep - so all 8,192 seeds of a class are decided by just
222 numbers, one per chunk. The best farm of a whole class falls out of
those 222 numbers directly. No seed is tried one by one.

So the search walks classes, not seeds: at each of the 10 most promising AFK
positions around a spot, every class of the top 5% of families (the ones
that can make the most chunks slime there). A cheap upper bound throws away
almost every class, and the exact calculation runs on the rest. The best
~600 layouts are then scored to the block with slime_refine, and anything
66+ is filed. About 13 seconds per spot on a laptop GPU.

Speed-ups are only accepted if they change NOTHING: identical output, row
for row, and the regression gate (heat\phase_regress.ps1) must re-find every
known farm. Faster but able to miss a farm = rejected.

================================================================================
 STEP 13 - What actually counts: the in-game number at y -39
================================================================================
Step 11 showed the model and the game can disagree. The fix turned out to be
simple geometry. The model scores with the player level with the floor. In
the reference rig you stand at y -39, 24 blocks above the slime floor - and at
exactly 24 blocks up, the 24-block no-spawn sphere just touches the floor.
The inner dead circle disappears (even the column straight below you counts),
while the outer circle shrinks from 128 to 125.7 blocks.

afk_rescan does that geometry. A full obsidian floor at -39 reads 49,669 in
game; afk_rescan says 49,669. For five measured farms its best AFK point
gives the in-game count exactly. That is why every 66+ farm also lands in
main\records\over66_y-39, named by that estimate: a farm with a modest model
cq but lots of slime near the middle can beat higher-cq farms in game -
the 69.39 did, and holds the in-game record at 18,598.

The game's number always wins. When a measurement disagrees with the
estimate (it did twice, by ~200), the estimate is the one that is wrong.
